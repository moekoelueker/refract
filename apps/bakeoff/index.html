Five ways to build glass on the web.

Same tokens, same specimen board, same eight backdrops. The only variable is the engine underneath. Every number below is measured, not estimated: contrast is sampled from the composited pixels, and refraction is proven by toggling depth and diffing the result.

Measured in Chromium at 1720×1040, 2026-07-24. Cross-engine parity is verified separately by the gate.

  • A2's conic sweep is expensive in Firefox. 197 ms p95 against 43 ms in Chromium. The mask-composite ring that makes the fake bevel convincing is the cost, so the default tier needs a Firefox-specific simplification or a cheaper rim.
  • WebKit is the fastest engine here, by a wide margin, for every approach. Even A3, which is the most expensive approach in the other two engines, holds 19 ms in WebKit. The received wisdom that Safari is the weak engine for glass is not what these numbers say.
  • All scroll figures are a deliberate stress case: eight simultaneous glass surfaces over a full-page scroll. Treat them as a ranking, not as a budget for one nav bar.

What I would ship, and why

Not one engine. A ladder of three, with the fourth rejected outright. The data does not support picking a single winner, and pretending otherwise would cost either the look or the frame budget.

A2 Edge is the default for volume surfaces. It carries most of the perceived premium for essentially no cost, works everywhere with no JavaScript, and is trivially restyleable. Cards, inputs, menus, badges, list rows: everything you have twenty of on a page.

A3 Refract is the flagship, capped to a handful of surfaces. It is the only approach that delivers genuine cross-engine refraction, which is the one claim nobody else in this space can make, and it looks the best on dark and photographic backdrops. But 89 ms to re-render eight surfaces and a 221 ms p95 during scroll means it must be rationed: heroes, a nav bar, a sheet, a travelling lens on a control. Not a grid of twenty cards.

A5 Shader is the media tier, and it is the surprise of this exercise. It scored the highest fidelity and the lowest scroll cost of all five, including the pure-CSS ones, because the GPU owns the work and scrolling never re-rasterises a filter. Its cost is elsewhere: it needs a texture rather than live DOM, and nothing inside a canvas is selectable, printable or reachable by a screen reader. So it earns video and canvas backdrops and dense scenes, not body copy.

A4 Native is rejected. It is strictly dominated: A3 does the same thing in three engines instead of one, with twice the rim movement. Its only genuine advantage is that it needs no declared backdrop, so it stays as a Chromium-only opt-in bonus, never a default.

The finding that changes the defaults

This one is not about engines at all.

  • Five of eight backdrops fail WCAG at the current dark-scheme tint of 0.16. Photographic and saturated backdrops sit at 1.9–3.3 against a requirement of 4.5. No engine fixes this, because it is a token problem, not a rendering problem.
  • Apple ships 0.8. Tutorial glassmorphism ships 0.1–0.2, and this bake-off just reproduced exactly why that is wrong. The default tint has to come up, and text has to sit on a content plate with a solved minimum alpha rather than directly on the material.
  • Note that A3 and A5 score better on contrast than the CSS approaches on dark backdrops, because their frost is a real Gaussian applied inside the engine rather than a composited backdrop-filter. Refraction is not only decoration here; it buys legibility.
  • A3's outlier is the light mesh at 2.14. The clone-plus-filter path brightens light backdrops more than the others, so the light scheme needs its own tint floor rather than a shared one.
